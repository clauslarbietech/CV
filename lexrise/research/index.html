Science & Research

How HERO uses dyslexia research—honestly labeled by evidence strength.

Evidence-BasedSupported by systematic reviews, meta-analyses, or established structured literacy guidance.
Evidence-InformedGrounded in research with mixed or context-dependent outcomes; benefits vary by learner.
ExperimentalEmerging or exploratory — not presented as established dyslexia treatment.

Research pillars

Phonological awareness & phonics

Evidence-Based

Dyslexia involves differences in phonological/language processing—not simply reversing letters. Structured Literacy builds sound awareness, blending, segmenting, and explicit decoding.

Morphological awareness

Evidence-Based

Older children and adults benefit from seeing words as meaningful pieces: un + help + ful, re + write, predict + ion. Included in Structured Literacy approaches.

Fluency & automaticity

Evidence-Informed

Move from 'figure this word out' toward efficient recognition via paced reading, repeated reading, and controlled challenges—without punishing slow reading.

Assistive technology

Evidence-Informed

Scan, Listen, and read-aloud tools can support adolescents and adults. Text-to-speech meta-analyses show modest comprehension benefits; results vary by person and tool.

Game-based learning

Evidence-Informed

Serious games can improve some phonological skills and engagement, but evidence is still relatively small. Adult dyslexia remains underserved in game-based research.

Neuroplasticity

Evidence-Informed

Reading intervention can accompany brain changes in reading networks, but findings are heterogeneous. Do not simplify to 'this game rewires your brain.'

Neurofeedback

Experimental

Current evidence does not establish significant reading benefits from neurofeedback alone for dyslexia. HERO takes UX inspiration—not clinical neurofeedback claims.

Emerging: AI, eye tracking, VR/AR

Experimental

AI-assisted screening, handwriting analysis, immersive tech, and neurostimulation are active research areas—not yet established treatments.

Detailed findings

Dyslexia is language processing—not letter reversal

Evidence-Based

Current research points to differences in phonological and language processing, reading networks, automaticity, and—in some individuals—visual/temporal processing, with substantial variation between people.

HERO builds around phonics, morphology, fluency, and assistive reading—not myths about reversed letters.

Fluency without punishment

Evidence-Informed

Automaticity develops through successful, repeated reading. Timed challenges can help some learners, but punishing slow reading increases anxiety and stigma.

HERO Reader Flow tracks completed passes, not speed scores.

Fluency / repeated reading literature

Letter spacing often helps more than specialty fonts

Evidence-Informed

Studies comparing Dyslexie and OpenDyslexic with common fonts often find little benefit from letter shapes alone. Spacing within and between words is a frequent reason readers improve.

In Reading Style, increase spacing first—then try fonts and keep what feels clearest.

The scrambled-text myth is not diagnostic

Evidence-Informed

Viral posts claim only people with dyslexia can read jumbled words. Most readers decode when first and last letters stay put. It is not a diagnostic test.

Scramble Challenge is for myth-busting fun—real progress comes from structured practice.
